Dennis was one of my first managers in the business. A largely fair man, a good egg, the kind of boss who'd remember your kids' names and buy the first round on a Friday without making a song and dance about it. But Dennis was also a man with obvious, glaring, neon-lit ambition. You could see it from orbit.

Dennis knew he was C-suite material. He *knew* it, the way some people know they'll win the lottery, or that the weird mole on their back is definitely nothing. For now, sure, he was a lowly analytics and reporting manager, buried under so many layers of corporate governance that you'd need a team of archaeologists and a decent set of trowels to find him on the org chart. But that was a temporary state of affairs. A clerical error. The universe would sort it out in time.

He knew, or at least thought he knew, what "good" looked like. And he had arrived at a theory, a simple and beautiful theory, that if you did enough "good" in the corporate world it would accrue, like interest in a savings account, until one day the great oak doors swung open and you were ushered into the holy of holies, anointed with oil, and handed a seat at the exec board table. Probably a leather one. Probably one that swivelled.

It is a lovely theory. I believed it myself, once. I'd like to believe it still.

## The Gospel of Good

You have to understand what "good" meant to Dennis, because it was a very specific liturgy, and he observed it with the devotion of a monk.

Good meant the monthly reports went out on time. Every time. Not a day late, not an hour late, not a minute past nine on the first working day of the month, even if it meant Dennis was sat in the office at eleven at night with a cold slice of pizza and a spreadsheet the size of a small European country.

Good meant never saying no to a request from upstairs. A director wanted a new cut of the churn numbers by Thursday? Done. A VP wanted the same dashboard in a different shade of blue for a board pack? Done, and with a choice of three blues. The CFO's assistant wanted help with a pivot table for the Christmas party budget? Dennis was there, sleeves rolled up, smiling like a man helping his favourite aunt move house.

Good meant loyalty. Showing up. Putting in the years. Being a safe pair of hands.

And Dennis was all of those things. Genuinely. He was a smart, canny manager who ran a tight ship and protected his people, and if the corporate world ran on merit, he'd have been a shoo-in.

Unfortunately, like many of my later career observations, I've come to learn that truth, justice and the American way are concepts wholly confined to comic books. There is no guarantee, let alone a marginal possibility, that being "good" within the walls of a tech company maps in time to being rewarded. Being good gets you more work. Being reliable gets you more work. Being a safe pair of hands gets you handed every flaming bag of nonsense nobody else wants to hold.

The people who get ushered through the oak doors are a different breed entirely. They're the ones who've been quietly lunching with the right people for years. The ones who know which board member's daughter is applying to which university. The ones who never once stayed late with a cold slice of pizza, because they were too busy at the bar with the people who decide who gets the swivelling chair.

Dennis believed in the gospel. And the trouble with believing in a gospel is that, sooner or later, you start expecting the miracle.

## The Loyalty Holiday

The company had a tradition. After five years of service, they'd bestow upon you a month's holiday and an extra month's salary as a reward for your "loyalty." A sabbatical, of sorts. A little pat on the head and a sugar cube for the faithful old horse who hadn't yet bolted for a competitor.

Five years in tech is a geological age. Most people never got near it. The ones who did were treated a bit like returning war veterans, people would ask what they were going to do with their month, and there'd be a cake, and someone from HR would make a short, faintly awkward speech about "commitment" and "the journey."

Dennis was coming up fast on his five years, and he had a plan. He was going to rent a boat and go sailing around Cape Cod, like some kind of long-lost Kennedy sibling. He talked about it for months. Charts on his desk. Tide tables. A new pair of deck shoes that he wore into the office one Friday, "to break them in," which none of us had the heart to mock to his face.

And off he went. Hyannis, Nantucket, the Vineyard. Salt air, open water, gulls wheeling overhead. Freedom.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about a month alone on a boat. There is an awful lot of *time*.

## Thirty Days of Rumination

Somewhere between singing sea shanties and fastening the yardarm, Dennis started to think.

He thought about the five years. He thought about the late nights and the cold pizza and the three shades of blue. He thought about the people he'd watched leapfrog him, the ones with the lunches and the golf handicaps and the remarkable gift for being in the room when the decisions got made. He thought about how much the reports team had grown under him, how the business couldn't function without the numbers he produced, how *indispensable* he'd become.

And alone out there on the Atlantic, with nobody to talk to but the gulls and a cooler full of Sam Adams, his thinking began to curdle.

The way I imagine it, it went something like this. Day one: *I've done a good job.* Day five: *I've done a really good job.* Day ten: *I've done a better job than most of the people above me.* Day fifteen: *Why am I not above them?* Day twenty: *They've been taking advantage of me.* Day twenty-five: *They know it, too.* Day thirty: *And when I get back, I'm going to make them fix it.*

It's a dangerous thing, a man alone with his grievances and a great deal of open water. It's how you get Captain Ahab. Dennis didn't have a white whale, but he had something worse. He had a *case*.

His ruminations concluded that he had been unfairly undervalued and mistreated by the company, and that this howling oversight would surely be corrected if only it were properly confronted and exposed. It wasn't that they didn't value him, you see. It was that nobody had ever *made* them. All he had to do was walk in, lay out the facts, and justice would roll down like waters.

So a month later, tanned and weather-beaten, salt still crusted in his eyebrows, Dennis came back from his vacation with the conviction of some anti-hero in a Western. A man who's been wronged. A man who's ridden into town at high noon, spurs jangling, looking for the sheriff.

## High Noon

We noticed it straight away. Dennis was *different*. There was a new set to his jaw. He'd stopped taking requests from upstairs with that eager, helpful smile. A director came over on his second day back asking for an ad hoc cut of something, and Dennis just looked at him, calmly, and said, "Put it in a ticket." The director walked away looking like he'd been slapped with a wet fish.

I didn't know, at the time, what was coming. I was young and stupid and assumed he'd had a nice holiday and found some inner peace. What he'd actually found was a loaded revolver.

The meeting happened behind closed doors, so what follows is pieced together from what Dennis told a couple of us afterwards over drinks, and what leaked out through the usual corporate plumbing. The broad strokes are clear enough.

Dennis went to his boss, and his boss's boss, and laid out his case. The years. The hours. The growth. The indispensability. And then he asked, plainly, for the promotion he felt he was owed.

Apparently the business held a rather different view to the one Dennis had arrived at while sailing circuits of Martha's Vineyard. They appreciated his work. Truly, they did. He was a valued member of the team. But the role he was asking for wasn't available, and frankly, he wasn't ready for it, and there was a process, and he needed to be patient.

In short, they told him to know his place.

And this, friends, is where a wiser man would have nodded, smiled, gone back to his desk, and quietly started updating his CV. Taken his grievance and turned it into leverage somewhere else. Walked out on his own terms, six months later, with a better title and a fatter salary from a competitor, and a polite LinkedIn post thanking everyone for "an incredible journey."

Dennis did not do that.

Dennis, still feeling the roll of the deck under his feet, still hearing the gulls, still riding high on thirty days of self-righteous sea air, issued an ultimatum. Promote me, or lose my services.

And the business didn't blink.

Not even a flutter. I don't think they so much as paused to take a sip of water. Because here's the cold, hard secret that every corporate ultimatum runs into, sooner or later, like a ship running onto the rocks in a fog: *nobody is indispensable*. Not you, not me, not Dennis, not the CEO. The reports would go out next month whether Dennis was there or not. Maybe a day late. Maybe in only one shade of blue. But they'd go out. And the people in that room knew it, even if Dennis had spent a month persuading himself otherwise.

An ultimatum only works if you're willing to lose, and if the other side is genuinely afraid of losing you. Dennis was willing. The business simply wasn't afraid. And a gun you point at someone who doesn't care if you pull the trigger isn't a threat. It's just a heavy thing you're holding.

## The Pop-Up

A couple of weeks later, we were all in a meeting room. Dennis was presenting something from his laptop, plugged into the big screen at the end of the table. I think it was the quarterly reporting roadmap. I genuinely don't remember. What I do remember, with a clarity that still makes me wince a decade and a half later, is what happened next.

A little notification popped up in the bottom corner of the screen. Right over the slides. Projected, eight feet wide, for the whole room to see.

It was from the HR manager. A cheery, efficient little message saying they'd meet later that day to finalise his departure date.

The room went silent. Not a quiet silence. A *loud* silence. The kind of silence that has a texture to it, that you can feel on the back of your neck. Eyes went to the screen, then to the table, then to the ceiling, then to absolutely anywhere that wasn't Dennis.

The team all saw it. Dennis saw that they saw it. And we all saw Dennis seeing that we saw it. It was a hall of mirrors, and in every mirror there was a man standing at the front of a meeting room with his career in flames behind him, eight feet wide, in a helpful sans-serif font.

To his eternal credit, Dennis closed the notification, cleared his throat, and finished the presentation. All of it. Every slide. I've thought about that a lot since. It was the bravest thing I ever saw him do, and also the saddest.

It was an ignominious end for a nice guy. A good manager, a decent human, but someone who simply could not read the room. Except that one day, in that one meeting, when he read it perfectly, and realised the horse had long since bolted, the stable door was swinging in the wind, and somebody from HR was already measuring up the stall for a new occupant.

## What I Learned From Dennis

I was young when this happened, and it rattled me. It was the first time I'd seen the machine up close, the moment where the friendly face of the company slips and you catch a glimpse of the gears underneath. I've seen it many times since, but you never forget the first one.

So for anyone out there currently drawing up charts and tide tables for their own sabbatical, here's what Dennis taught me, at his own expense.

**Never make career decisions alone at sea.** Or on a beach, or up a mountain, or in any other place where the only voice you can hear is your own. Rumination isn't reflection. It's a feedback loop, and it'll always tell you what you want to hear. Before you march in with a grievance, run it past someone who'll tell you the truth, ideally someone who doesn't love you very much.

**Being good is not the same as being promoted.** Good work is the price of admission, not the prize. The people deciding who goes through the oak doors aren't reading your monthly reports. They're asking who they know, who they trust, and who's been in the room. If you want the seat, you have to build the relationships that put you in it, and you have to do it years before you ask.

**Never issue an ultimatum you're not prepared to lose.** And if you're prepared to lose it, you don't need to issue it at all. Just go. Get the offer elsewhere first. An ultimatum with an offer letter in your back pocket is a negotiation. An ultimatum without one is a resignation letter you haven't typed yet.

**Nobody is indispensable.** I cannot stress this enough. The graveyards are full of indispensable men, and so are the LinkedIn feeds. The business will route around you like water around a rock, and within six months, there'll be people sat in your old meetings who never knew you existed.

**And for the love of God, turn off your notifications before you share your screen.** Every single time. Forever. It is the cheapest insurance policy in the modern workplace, and Dennis paid the full premium so you don't have to.

I lost touch with Dennis not long after he left. Last I heard, he'd landed on his feet somewhere, managing a team at a smaller outfit, which is what happens to most good people who get spat out of the machine. He'll have been fine. Better than fine, probably. Good eggs usually are.

But I still think about him every time someone tells me they're "owed" a promotion. Every time I see someone come back from a long holiday with that particular glint in their eye, that high-noon squint, that look of a man who's spent too long talking to the gulls.

I want to grab them by the shoulders and say: *I know. I know you're right. I know they don't deserve you.*

*Now go back to your desk, smile, and update your CV.*
