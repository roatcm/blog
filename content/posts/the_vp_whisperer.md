Every org has one. If you've been in the game long enough, you've met them, and if you think your org doesn't have one, I have bad news for you, friend. It's probably you. I'm talking about the VP Whisperer. The courtier. The self-appointed keeper of the royal ear.

We'll call this one Casey. That isn't their name, and they'd be thrilled to know they'd made it into print, which is precisely why the real one stays locked in the drawer with the other unexploded ordnance.

Casey had a gift. Not for building anything, God knows. Not for leading a team, or shipping a product, or understanding the first thing about the data their own people were wrangling. No. Casey's gift was for *proximity*. For standing close enough to power that some of its glow rubbed off, the way a moth gets a little dusting of light right before it hits the bulb.

In any meeting Casey turned up to, and they turned up to a great many meetings, most of them uninvited, they would appoint themselves the mouthpiece of the VP. Not the VP's deputy. Not the VP's representative. The VP's *voice*, channelled, like a medium at a Victorian séance with the lights down low and someone kicking the table from underneath.

"Well, I was talking to [VP] about this only yesterday..."

"[VP]'s view, and I think I can speak for them here..."

"When [VP] and I were chatting over coffee, they mentioned..."

The name got dropped so often it left dents in the conference table. And always, always, the implication that there had been a private audience, a closed-door session, a little fireside tête-à-tête where the boudoir door had been flung wide open and the secrets of the boudoir laid bare for Casey, and Casey alone. The rest of us were peasants pressing our faces to the palace railings. Casey had been *inside*.

Whether any of these conversations had happened in the form described is a question I could never answer. I suspect some of them were real. I suspect a great many more were a thirty-second exchange by the lift that had been inflated, in the retelling, into a Camp David summit. That's the beauty of the con. Nobody's going to march up to a VP and ask, "Did you actually say that, or is Casey running a ventriloquist act with your face?" Nobody has that kind of career death wish.

Casey spent their every waking hour "getting ahead." I put it in quotes because that's how they'd say it, with the earnest little glow of someone describing a spiritual practice. Getting ahead with senior management. Getting ahead of the narrative. Getting ahead of the rest of us, mainly, which they seemed to think was the same thing as doing a good job.

And in that pursuit, there was one thing that infuriated Casey above all others. It wasn't missed deadlines, or broken pipelines, or a customer screaming down the phone. It was my calendar.

Specifically, my rule. A simple rule, and I'll defend it to my grave: if you want to put a meeting in my calendar, it comes with an agenda. Two lines will do. One, even. "Discuss Q3 reporting handoff." "Review the forecasting model." Anything. Just tell me what we're there for.

You'd think I'd asked them to donate a kidney.

Because an agenda is a commitment. An agenda is a paper trail. An agenda means you have to state, in advance, in writing, what you want. And the entire Casey method depended on *never* stating what they wanted. The ambush only works if the target doesn't know it's an ambush. Ask a rattlesnake to file a written notice of intent to strike and you'll find it suddenly loses all interest in the conversation.

So the meeting invites would arrive blank. "Quick sync." "Catch up." "Touch base." I'd decline and ask for an agenda. They'd go over my head and complain that I was "difficult to engage with." Which, when you think about it, was the most accurate thing Casey ever said about me.

Here's the thing you learn about operators like Casey, if you spend enough time watching them work. What they *don't* say in a private meeting is every bit as important as what they do.

The words coming out of their mouth were mostly misdirection. A magician's patter. Look over here, at this harmless question about roadmap alignment, while the other hand is reaching into your pocket for your team's work. You'd come out of a one-on-one with Casey feeling vaguely like you'd had a pleasant chat, and then three days later you'd realise you'd been pickpocketed, and that the thing they'd been fishing for was buried in a throwaway line at the eleventh minute.

There was always a scheme. Something properly Machiavellian, cooked up in the dark with all the slow patience of a stew left on the hob overnight. And the scheme was almost always the same: stake a claim on someone else's work, and plant a flag on it before anyone noticed.

The grand ambition, as far as I could ever tell, was to install themselves in some kind of oversight role. Over me. Over my team. Over, ideally, anyone whose work might one day produce a slide that could be shown upstairs. Casey didn't want to build the railroad. Casey wanted to stand at the junction and wave the little flag and have everyone think they'd laid the track.

The day I truly understood what I was dealing with, it was over a piece of work my team was doing that was, frankly, the most promising thing to come out of that department in a long time.

It was good. Properly good. The kind of early-stage work where you can feel the thing taking shape under your hands, where the numbers start lining up and everyone on the team starts staying an extra hour not because they've been told to, but because they want to see what happens next. If you've been lucky enough to have that feeling in your career, you know how rare it is. If you haven't, I'm sorry. Keep looking.

Casey noticed it too. Of course they did. They had a nose for kudos the way a truffle pig has a nose for fungus.

And so, in a meeting with the higher-ups, Casey stood up and pronounced, with great solemnity and a face like an undertaker measuring a widow, that my team's work was *duplicative*. Duplicative! Of something Casey's own team had built.

Now, the thing Casey's team had built did exist. I'll give them that. It existed in the way a three-legged chair exists. You could point at it. You could say, "That is a chair." But nobody in their right mind would sit on it, and the people who'd tried had ended up on the floor with a bruised coccyx and a look of quiet betrayal. It was a poor fit for the problem. It had always been a poor fit for the problem. Everybody who'd ever used it knew it.

But that wasn't the point. The point was never whether my team's work was duplicative. The point was that my team was, in Casey's mind, a *rival*. And rivals who produce good work are rivals who get credit. And credit, in Casey's universe, was a fixed pie, and every slice my team took was a slice stolen from their plate.

So the play was simple. Get it shut down. Strangle it in the crib. If you can't take the credit, make sure no one gets it.

I've seen a lot of low moves in my time. That one still sits with me. Not because it was clever. Because it was so nakedly, shamelessly *small*.

Any interaction with Casey, from that point on, had to be handled with the delicacy you'd use on a rattlesnake you'd found coiled up under your parlour sofa. Or a particularly odious skunk that had wandered in through the cat flap and was now eyeing the good rug. Slow movements. No sudden noises. Don't turn your back. And whatever you do, never, ever give it something to work with.

Because Casey had a signature move. I came to know it as well as I know the sound of my own alarm clock, and I dreaded it about as much.

It went like this.

A private message would arrive. Friendly. Casual. Out of the blue. Something like:

*"Hey! Quick one. Where do you see your team's responsibilities in terms of supporting sales?"*

Innocent enough, right? The kind of question a colleague might ask while trying to understand the lay of the land. And so, the first time, I answered it the way you'd answer any reasonable question from a reasonable person. Matter-of-factly. Honestly. Here's what we do, here's where we stop, here's where we hand off.

And then, *blammo*.

Within minutes, sometimes within seconds, a brand-new group chat would spring into existence like a mushroom after rain. And in it, the higher-ups. The VP. A director or two. Whoever had the most authority and the least context. And there was Casey, front and centre, announcing to the assembled court that *their* team owned everything to do with supporting sales, that there had clearly been some confusion, and that the sensible thing, the grown-up thing, the *aligned* thing, would be for my team to hand over the keys immediately.

My private, good-faith answer had been the bait. The group chat was the trap. And the whole time, Casey had known exactly what they were going to do with whatever I said. It didn't matter what my answer was. The answer was just the excuse to pull the trigger in front of an audience.

What a tool.

The first time it happened, I sat staring at my screen with the slow, cold, crawling feeling of a man who has just realised the friendly stranger who offered to help with his bags at the train station has also, somehow, taken his wallet.

The second time, I was ready.

Here's what I learned, the hard way, for anyone out there currently trapped in a building with their own Casey. Write this on the inside of your eyelids.

**Never answer a scope question in private.** If someone DMs you asking where your team's responsibilities begin and end, the correct answer is: "Great question. Let's get it on the agenda for the next staff meeting so everyone's aligned." Then watch them squirm. A private question about ownership is never just a question. It's a deposition.

**Insist on agendas. Forever.** Yes, they'll call you difficult. Wear it like a medal. The agenda is your Kevlar vest. It forces the operator to declare intent in writing, and an operator forced to declare intent is an operator with no ambush left.

**Take the name-dropping at face value, out loud.** When someone says "[VP] thinks we should...", the answer is: "Oh, great. Let's loop [VP] in to confirm, so we're all working off the same page." You'd be amazed how quickly the VP's opinions evaporate when there's a risk of the actual VP being asked about them. Ventriloquists hate it when the dummy talks back.

**Document your team's work early and publicly.** Good work done quietly is good work waiting to be stolen. Demo it. Write it up. Put your team's names on it in big letters where the higher-ups can see. A flag already planted is a lot harder to pull up.

**And watch what they don't say.** Every Casey meeting has a hidden item. Your job is to find it before it finds you.

I'd love to tell you there was a grand comeuppance. That Casey's three-legged chair finally collapsed under the weight of a board presentation, that the VP turned to them one day and said, "I've never had a coffee with you in my life," and that security escorted them off the premises while the whole floor stood and applauded.

It didn't go like that. It rarely does. Operators like Casey don't fall so much as drift. They float upward on a thermal of other people's work until they reach some altitude where nobody can quite see what they do anymore, and then they hover there, forever, issuing opinions on behalf of people more senior than themselves.

But my team's work survived. It shipped. It's still running, last I heard, quietly doing the job it was built for, long after the duplicative claims and the ambush group chats have faded into the great grey static of Slack history. Nobody remembers who tried to kill it. Everybody remembers who built it.

That's the only revenge worth having, in the end. Not a public execution. Just the long, slow, satisfying fact of being right.

And Casey, if you're reading this, and you are, because people like you always find the thing written about them:

Please send me an agenda first.
