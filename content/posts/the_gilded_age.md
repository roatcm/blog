I am old enough now to have survived several hype cycles, and I use the word *survived* the way a man uses it after he has walked out of a burning pub with his pint still in his hand. Web 2.0. The Mobile Internet. Big Data, which was mostly small data with a large invoice. And God help us all, the Metaverse, which was a cartoon of a meeting room that cost more than the actual meeting room. They come and they go, these things, like rain in Galway, but they all wash in on the one great filthy tide, and the name of that tide is hubris.

But there was one stretch of road, one long, glittering, neon-lit stretch of road, that stands out above all the others for its waste, its spending, and its sheer brass-necked extravagance. It ran from about 2016 to 2020, and anyone who was there and tells you they remember it clearly was either sober, which is suspicious, or lying, which is worse.

This was the Gilded Age. And I was in the belly of it.

---

## The Parties

There were office parties every week. *Every week.* Not the miserable class of a thing where a manager from Finance cuts a sheet cake for somebody's leaving and everyone stands around holding paper plates like mourners at a wake where the corpse owes them money. No. These were proper affairs, with kegs and a DJ and a bar staffed by people paid specifically to pour.

And that was only the opening act. Around eleven o'clock the more boisterous element, and there was always a more boisterous element, would peel off into the Manhattan night like a squadron of drunken Spitfires. They would go clubbing. They would meet friends. They would meet friends of friends, and strangers, and people who were possibly not people at all. And then, at some hour God never intended, the whole howling caravan would come back to the office for the after-party.

Back to the office. Think about that. Grown adults, with mortgages and pension contributions, choosing of their own free will to go back to the place where they worked, at three in the morning, to keep drinking. My grandfather would have walked forty miles in the rain to get *away* from his place of work. These people were going back to it in Ubers, singing.

The security guard on the night desk, a decent man from the Bronx who had seen things in his time, used to watch them come through the turnstiles with the thousand-yard stare of a fella who knows his pension is not worth what he is about to witness.

## The Canteen

Then there was the canteen. It was open. I don't mean open as in it had opening hours. I mean open the way the gates of heaven are said to be open, though with better coffee and fewer questions asked at the door.

Friends were fed there. Friends of friends were fed there. Men I had never once seen do a day's work in the building were fed there, regularly, and with great appetite, through the munificence of the cowardly tech oligarchs who ruled over us all like absentee landlords in fleece vests. There was a lad who I'm fairly sure didn't work for the company at all, didn't work anywhere at all, who came in three days a week for the poke bowls. Nobody stopped him. Nobody *could* stop him. Stopping him would have required someone to know who was supposed to be there, and nobody knew that. That information was above all our pay grades, and possibly above the pay grade of the CEO, who was in Davos.

## The Dairy Heist

You want to know how far the rot went? I'll tell you how far.

There was a product designer. Lowly enough in the great chain of being, but a member, mind you, of that most exclusive and self-regarding club, the *product triad*, which is to say she was one of the three people in the room who got to point at a whiteboard while everyone else nodded. And every single weekend this woman would charge a taxi to the company account, ride across the city to the empty office, and strip it clean. Milk. Yoghurt. Cheese. The good crackers, the ones with the seeds in them. Whole armfuls of snacks, bags of them, bundled into the back of a cab like contraband rum during Prohibition. All of it bound for the fridge she shared with her housemates.

She was running a dairy smuggling operation out of a Fortune 500 company, and she was expensing the getaway car.

I'll say this for her, it took them an astonishingly long time to notice. She was fired eventually, of course. But I've known men to do six months in Mountjoy for less, and here was this one, operating in broad daylight for a year with the full logistical support of the accounts payable department. There's a certain genius in it. A terrible, lactose-heavy genius.

## The Swag

And the swag. Sweet suffering Jesus, the swag.

T-shirts. Hoodies. Tote bags by the thousand, tote bags enough to carry the whole of the Irish Sea home in. Branded water bottles. Branded socks. Branded *sneakers*, which nobody wore, because a grown man in a pair of runners with a corporate logo on the heel looks like he was released from a cult on a technicality. Lanyards. Stickers. Phone stands shaped like the mascot. Little stress balls shaped like the mascot, which were squeezed, I'd imagine, in great quantities, by the people in Finance watching all this stuff eat and eat and *eat* into the socials budget like a plague of locusts with a corporate card.

I had a drawer at home I couldn't open. It was full of fleece. I'd try to open it and it would push back. There was a sort of pressure in there, a kind of sartorial geology, layer upon layer of quarter-zip pullovers from offsites I don't remember attending.

## The Holiday Parties

Christmas was the high holy day, the feast of the Golden Calf. Entire venues were booked out, not rooms, *venues*, buildings that normally hosted boxing matches and Billy Joel, for a few thousand people at a time. And the drink. The drink and the food came pouring out in a torrent of waste so violent you'd have thought it was the last night before the Flood and somebody'd tipped off the caterers.

I saw an ice sculpture of the company logo melting into a tray of oysters at one in the morning, and I remember thinking, with a clarity that only the fourth gin can provide, *this is it. This is the whole thing. This is the entire economy in one frozen, dripping, shellfish-scented metaphor.* I tried to explain this to a solutions architect from Denver. He told me to try the sliders.

And that was just the holidays. Every weekend there was a free tab at the local bar, and it was there that the new hires were taught, as reliably as any catechism, to suck at the teat of free booze. You could watch it happen. A young one would come in on a Monday, bright-eyed, a bit nervous, ordering a sparkling water. By the following Friday he'd be at the bar with his badge still on, ordering a round of Negronis for people he'd met forty minutes before, and he'd never once look at the bill, because there was no bill. There was never a bill. That was the great lie at the heart of the whole thing.

## The Summits

But if you want to see the beast in its natural habitat, really see it, rearing up on its hind legs and roaring, you have to go to the product summits.

I went to several. I'm not proud of it. I'm not ashamed of it either, I'm just reporting the facts as a man who was in the trenches with a lanyard round his neck.

Picture it. A ballroom in a hotel, the carpet the colour of a migraine. Hundreds of product managers and sales team leads, coked to the gills, hooting like owls in a bushfire. Two-handed overhead high fives. *Chest bumps.* Grown men in vests and grown women in vests, bumping chests, as if a quarterly roadmap was a touchdown in the Super Bowl. And in between all the bumping they'd be grabbing at the corporate-branded donuts, the corporate-branded drinks flasks, the corporate-branded anything, with the wild, darting eyes of people who suspected, somewhere deep down in the reptile part of the brain, that it could all stop at any moment.

The keynote speaker was usually a man who'd written a book about leadership. The book was usually about himself. He'd pace the stage in a headset microphone and say words like *synergy* and *flywheel* and *north star*, and the crowd would roar, and I'd sit at the back with a cold coffee and a growing sense that I'd wandered into some kind of tent revival where the god being worshipped was a hockey-stick graph.

I could go on. God knows I could go on. But my solicitor advises me otherwise.

## The Rats

It got so bad at one point, so gloriously, biblically bad, that the City of New York itself threatened to slap the company with a fine.

Not for tax evasion. Not for some grand, cinematic financial crime. For *rats*.

All the food waste in the basement, the uneaten poke bowls and the untouched cheese boards and the mountains of seeded crackers the dairy bandit hadn't got round to yet, had attracted a small army of rats. Not a few rats. An *army*. A standing, organised, well-fed force of rats, the best-nourished rodents in the five boroughs. Glossy rats. Confident rats. Rats who'd been eating artisanal sourdough for two years and had developed opinions about it.

And the fine itself was hefty, by the standards of normal people, and a rounding error by the standards of the company, who could have paid it out of the budget for the branded socks and not noticed. That was the most obscene part of the whole thing. Not that they were wasteful. That they were so rich the waste didn't even *register*. The rats were eating better than the people cleaning up after them, and the people at the top couldn't have told you there was a basement.

## The Morning After

And then it ended, the way these things always end. Not with a bang but with a Zoom call. 2020 came in like a bailiff, and the parties stopped, and the canteen shut, and the free tab was closed, and the summits went "virtual", which is a word that means the same thing as "cancelled" but takes longer to say. Then the money got dear again, and the layoffs came in waves, and the same oligarchs who'd fed the whole city on poke bowls discovered, quite suddenly, a deep and spiritual love of *efficiency*.

I don't miss it, exactly. You don't miss a fever. But I think about it sometimes, late at night, the way an old soldier thinks about a war he didn't believe in. It was a strange and stupid and wonderful time, built on nothing more than cheap money and the collective delusion of very clever people, and it was never, ever going to last.

They never do. The tide goes out. The rats move on. And somewhere, I promise you, in a drawer in Brooklyn, there's a pair of branded sneakers that's never been worn, waiting patiently for the next Gilded Age to come round.

It will. It always does. It's probably already here, and it's wearing a vest with an AI logo on it.
