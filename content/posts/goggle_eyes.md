A few years after starting out in the business systems division, I was free and easy in the most dangerous sense of the axiom, I had more money than I knew what to do with, no mortgage, no dependents, and absolutely no idea what I was about. I was the kind of young man who could be talked into almost anything by a sufficiently confident person with a corporate card. So when the company opened a shiny new European office in Germany, and the boss leaned over my desk one grey Thursday afternoon and asked if I knew anyone who’d fancy spending some time out there to help get them up and running, I didn’t so much volunteer as lunge. Visions of European fun and frolics on the company tab danced in my head like sugarplums laced with cheap amphetamine. Long lunches. Late nights. Expense reports written in a language I could pretend not to understand. I signed up there and then, before he’d even finished the sentence.

What followed was the strangest year of my life, and I’ve had some strange years.

Berlin in those days was a city still figuring out what it wanted to be, a vast sprawling stage set of crumbling plaster, raw concrete and techno basements where the night didn’t end so much as gradually dissolve into Tuesday. I arrived with two suitcases and a head full of nonsense and was installed in a red-bricked alleyway office that smelled of fresh carpet and venture capital. And presiding over it all, like some malfunctioning animatronic from a theme park nobody visits anymore, was the senior VP.

He was a goggle-eyed German version of Mike Nesmith from The Monkees, all lank hair and woollen-hat energy, but with eyes that bulged behind thick lenses like two boiled eggs pressed against an aquarium wall. Those eyes never blinked at the right moments. They tracked you across a room with the patient, unhurried interest of a heron watching a pond. I became convinced, within my first fortnight, that the man had no soul whatsoever, that somewhere along the way it had been surgically removed and replaced with a quarterly revenue target, and that if left unchecked he would simply move from evil deed to evil deed with the mechanical regularity of a Teutonic cuckoo clock, emerging on the hour to do something terrible, then retreating back inside to wait.

The business, as these businesses always are, was selling stuff to people who had never wanted to buy it in the first place. This is a peculiar trade when you stop to examine it, which almost nobody in it ever does. It means hunting down the people who do not want your product, finding out everything you possibly can about them, and then using that knowledge to wear them down like water on limestone until they reach for their credit card in a kind of exhausted trance. And in that trade, data is king, queen, and the entire royal household. Which brought us, inevitably, into the orbit of the one great evil behemoth of social media, the past masters of harvesting every click, every like, every lonely 2 a.m. scroll, and then selling it on with all the pomp and ceremony of a royal wedding to people exactly like us.

The day their people came to visit was the day I first understood what kind of place I’d landed in. Three of them arrived in matching fleece gilets, gleaming with that particular West Coast health that suggests a diet of kale, cold brew and stock options. The meeting itself was a blur of slide decks and acronyms, audience segments and lookalike models, the dull liturgy of the surveillance economy. But it was what happened after that I’ll never forget. The meeting ended, and old Goggle Eyes marched out of the room, swivelled those terrible eyes across the open-plan floor like a lighthouse sweeping for wrecks, and began pointing at women sitting at their desks. “You. And you. Not you. You. Come with us now.” Just like that. They were to accompany these shills from the social media company to dinner and drinks, as decoration, as sweeteners, as so much eye candy laid out on the table to grease the wheels of the deal.

I was utterly stunned. I’d been in the business long enough to know it was grubby, but this was something else entirely, something so naked and medieval that it felt like I’d stumbled through a wormhole into a court of some petty Habsburg archduke picking out courtesans for visiting envoys. The women in question exchanged looks I’ve never been able to fully read, a mix of weariness, calculation and something closer to fury, and then they picked up their coats because that’s what you did, because he was the VP and this was Berlin and the deal had to close. I stood there by the photocopier with my mouth open like a landed carp, and a cold certainty settled in my gut that this place was rotten from the foundations up.

That was the moment I stopped being a tourist and started being a nuisance.

My eventual fault, in his eyes, was asking too many questions, and in particular asking them of the people in the finance team. It started innocently enough. A number on a spreadsheet that didn’t quite add up. A vendor I’d never heard of with a suspiciously large invoice. A line item labelled “client entertainment” that was big enough to entertain a small nation. I would wander over to finance with a coffee and a puzzled expression and ask, as politely as a young idiot can, what exactly that was for. The answers got vaguer. The silences got longer. The finance people started finding urgent reasons to be elsewhere whenever they saw me coming across the floor, scattering like pigeons before a toddler.

And then one morning I looked up from my desk to find those boiled-egg eyes fixed on me from across the office, unblinking, utterly still, the heron finally noticing that the fish in the pond had started asking where all the other fish had gone. I knew right then that my European adventure was about to take a very different turn, and that whatever came next, it would not be on the company tab.
