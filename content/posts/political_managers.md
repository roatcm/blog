I have worked under a great many managers in my time, and I have come to think of them the way an old sailor thinks of weather. Some are mild and kind and hopelessly out of their depth, drifting along on good intentions like a rowing boat with one oar. Some care for nothing at all except themselves, their bonus, and the shine on their next job title. And then there is a third kind, a darker kind, the kind that rolls in low over the horizon with a greenish tinge to it and makes the dogs howl. Somewhere between malign and outright evil.

The specimen I want to describe here lived on the border between the second and the third kind. He was malign, yes, but mostly in service of his own self-regard, the way a tapeworm is malign. It has nothing personal against you. It just wants to eat.

He was a portly, smug little bastard, and I'll call him Mikey. Names have been changed to protect the guilty.

The first time I understood exactly what I was dealing with, it was over a notebook.

We were in a meeting. One of those long, airless, windowless meetings in a room named after a mountain or a river or some other natural feature the occupants would never live to see, because they would be in this room forever. Mikey was holding forth. He was always holding forth. He had the cadence of a man who believed every sentence leaving his mouth should be carved into marble and sent to the Smithsonian.

And at some point in the middle of this oration, he stopped. He looked at me. And his little face went the colour of a boiled ham.

"Where's your notebook?"

I did not have a notebook. I had a laptop, which was open, and a brain, which was also, at that point, mostly open. But I did not have a notebook and pen on hand to dutifully transcribe his sayings, and this, apparently, was an outrage of the highest order. He confronted me about it right there, in front of everyone, with a sort of trembling fury, like a minor prophet who has caught a shepherd boy dozing during the Sermon on the Mount.

I sat there and I looked at him and something clicked in my head, the way a gun clicks when you realise it is pointing at you. I had seen this man before. Not in person. In grainy footage. Fat little dictators standing on balconies, waving at crowds who had been bussed in under threat of the camps, while men in uniforms scribbled furiously in their little notebooks to capture every pearl of wisdom. Chairman Mao and the Little Red Book. And that other one, the round little fella from North Korea with the haircut, who Mikey resembled to a degree that frankly should have been reported to someone.

That was the moment I knew. This was not a manager. This was a cult of personality with a company-issued laptop bag and a badge to get in the door.

Mikey's tricks were not sophisticated. I want to be clear about that. There was no dark genius at work here, no Machiavelli in a poorly fitted shirt. His methods were standard-issue, off-the-rack, the kind of political manoeuvres you could pick up in any airport bookshop between the neck pillows and the Toblerone.

But they *worked*. Lord help us, they worked. And they worked for one reason only - the place had absolutely no structure. No order. No logic, no rigour, no grown-up thinking behind any of its business reporting. Numbers floated around the building on printed excel pages like smoke in a pub before the ban, and nobody could tell you where they had come from or what they meant. In that kind of fog, a man who walks in carrying anything that looks like a map is crowned king.

So Mikey made maps.

He pulled out a handful of reports. Revenue attribution. Deal close rate changes. A few other bits of shiny, laminated nonsense. Whether any of it was true was a question no one thought to ask, because asking would have required understanding, and understanding was in very short supply on the executive floor. He carried these reports upstairs to the leadership team, who were by and large as clueless as a herd of cattle staring at a new gate.

And he presented them the way Moses must have presented the stone tablets. Coming down off the mountain with his robes flapping, his eyes ablaze, the voice of the Almighty still ringing in his ears. *Behold. Here is your revenue attribution. Thou shalt not question the methodology.*

They loved it. They lapped it up like dogs at a spilled pint. A BI Moses had come among them, and they would follow him wherever he wished to lead them, which, it turned out, was mostly towards a larger budget for Mikey.

Now here is something you'll learn about the political manager if you spend enough years in the trenches. They never come alone. They are not lone wolves. They are more like a particular kind of ant that marches into a new colony, kills the queen, and then sends word back home for the rest of the family to come and move in.

Every political operator has a little band of trusted sycophants trailing behind him from his or her previous company. Old comrades. Potential Nuremburg co-accused. Men and women who have been laughing at his jokes for so long that their faces have set that way, like the wind changed on them. And the moment he gets his feet under the desk, the moment he has a sniff of real power, he starts installing them in roles way above their station.

So it was with Mikey. Somebody, in a moment of madness I will never fully understand, handed him the keys to the department budget. And he took that money, that great warm pile of operating expenditure, and he spent most of it hiring a crew of swinish lieutenants from his old company. Then he farmed them out across the organisation to manage other teams, like a Roman general dispatching loyal centurions to keep an eye on the provinces.

They were not good at their jobs. That was never the point. The point was that they were *his* creatures. Every team they ran became another little outpost of the Mikey empire, another set of ears, another pair of hands to carry his tablets up the mountain. You'd go to a meeting with some team you'd never worked with before and there, at the head of the table, would be one of them, smiling the same smile, using the same phrases, with the same faint whiff of the old company about them. It was like a horror film where the whole town slowly gets replaced by pod people, except the pods were expensed.

And then, as is the way with these things, the end came quietly.

For his final month or so, Mikey went into hiding. Not literally, not with a false beard and a forged passport, though I wouldn't have put it past him. He simply took up residence in an upstairs meeting room, door closed, blinds half-drawn, and stayed there for hours at a stretch, on what he described to anyone who asked as "some important calls."

Important calls. We all nodded. We imagined he was in there with the CEO, with the board, with the bankers, plotting the next great leap forward. Some of the lieutenants walked a little taller that month. Something big was coming.

Something big *was* coming. Just not for us.

What Mikey was actually doing up there, in that sealed room with its humming air conditioner and its whiteboard full of someone else's half-erased diagrams, was negotiating his way out. A telecoms provider in the Middle East. A big job, a big title, and most importantly, a big, fat, tax-free salary.

You have to understand the appeal of this to a man like Mikey. It was the purest expression of everything he was. The greedy little bastard wanted to squeeze that tax-free sponge until it ran dry, and he had a wife who was every bit his equal in that department, a woman whose eyes, I'm told, lit up at the phrase *expense account* like a slot machine paying out. The plan was simple and grotesque in its simplicity. Pack up the kids, ship them out to a walled expat compound with a swimming pool and armed guards at the gate, and live there in a bubble of air conditioning and duty-free gin for a few years. Bank the cash. Then come home and retire early, somewhere suitably waspish up-state, with a lawn and a flagpole and a golf club that would have him.

A few years. That was the plan.

The last time I checked, it has been ten years. Maybe more. And he's still out there. Still squeezing. Still ducking the occasional Iranian drone, and goodness knows what else, to suckle at the teat of tax-free money. Somewhere in a gleaming tower on the edge of the desert, I like to think, there is a young analyst who has just been screamed at for not bringing a notebook.

There is a tradition, in the places I have worked, that when someone leaves you all go down to the local bar for going-away drinks. It doesn't matter much whether you liked them. You go. You stand around. You buy a round, you say a few nice words, you tell a story or two that makes them look better than they were. It is a small kindness, a ritual, the corporate equivalent of throwing a handful of earth on the coffin.

Mikey had his going-away drinks.

Not one of his direct reports turned up. Not one. Not me, and not a single other member of the BI team that had laboured under him all those long months. The lieutenants were there, I'm told, huddled together at one end of the bar like the last loyalists in the bunker. But the people who had actually worked for him, the ones who had built the reports he carried up the mountain, the ones he had shouted at over notebooks, stayed away. We were somewhere else. Probably in a different bar. Possibly in the same bar, at the other end, in the dark, with our backs turned.

I can think of no more honest performance review than that. An empty room speaks louder than any 360-degree feedback form ever printed.

As for me, I waited. I waited until his access card had been safely handed back to security, until the turnstile had clicked shut behind him for the last time, until I was quite sure that he could never again summon me to a room named after a river. And then I opened up LinkedIn, found his smug little face, and hit *Block*.

It was one of the most satisfying clicks of my professional life. I haven't had to think about him since.

Well. Until now.
