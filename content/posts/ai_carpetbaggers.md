We were somewhere around the third keynote of the morning when the panic began to take hold. I remember saying something like "I feel a bit lightheaded, maybe you should drive the slide clicker", and then suddenly there was a terrible roar all around us and the ballroom was full of what looked like huge, bald, quarter-zipped bats, all swooping and screeching and diving at the stage, and a voice was screaming "AGENTIC! AGENTIC! IT'S ALL AGENTIC NOW!"

It was not, of course, a hallucination. Not mine, anyway. It was the Fractional Chief AI Officer. Dozens of them. Hundreds, possibly, it was impossible to count the things because they moved in a flock, from the coffee station to the networking zone to the free-lanyard table, honking about transformation and readiness and the urgent need for an AI strategy before the competition eats your lunch, your dinner, and the Tupperware you brought them in.

I had come to this place, a beige hotel conference centre on the ragged outskirts of a city that will remain nameless for legal reasons, because I had been told there was money in the air. And there was. You could smell it. That sour, sweaty, electric smell of a gold rush in its late stages, when the actual gold is long gone and the only people still making a living are the ones selling shovels, maps, and seminars on The Seven Habits of Highly Effective Prospectors.

## The Laid-Off and the Damned

Here is the shape of it, for those who have been lucky enough to avoid it. It seems that if you are a recently laid-off tech executive, perhaps somewhat responsible for your own situation due to an effusion of hype over technical reality, your next role, your natural and inevitable next role, as sure as water runs downhill and into the basement, is as an AI consultancy. You will tell morons how to "get AI done right in their business", or some other such nonsense, and you will charge them by the day for the privilege of hearing it.

These are not bad people, exactly. Or rather, they are not bad people in any way that would stand up in court. They are something worse, something more modern. They are hollow people. They are the end product of a long and grinding process of corporate erosion known as the Peter Principle, whereby a person fails upwards gradually enough that whatever positive qualities or genuine knowledge that started them on this road has atrophied, withered, and dropped off like a dead toenail. Somewhere back there, twenty years ago, there was a young engineer who could actually build a thing. A database. A network. A small, honest piece of software that did one job and did it well. But then came the promotion to team lead, and then manager, and then director, and then VP of Something Strategic, and with each step up the ladder another chunk of competence fell off into the void, until what you are left with is a man in a gilet who started out as something that no longer exists and now does something that never existed in the first place.

Up and over and out. And then, with the severance cheque still warm in the bank, straight onto LinkedIn.

## The LinkedIn Fever Dream

I have spent time in some very strange places. I have been in dive bars at four in the morning where the bartender kept a hatchet under the counter and was not shy about mentioning it. I have sat through budget meetings where grown men wept openly over headcount. But nothing, nothing in my experience, prepared me for the sheer psychic violence of the LinkedIn feed of a freshly minted AI thought leader.

It starts with the announcement. "Thrilled to share that after an incredible 11 years, I'm embarking on a new chapter." Two hundred likes. Forty comments, all of them some variation of "Congrats, they're lucky to have you!" posted by people who have not read the post and do not know who "they" are, because "they" do not exist. There is no "they". There is a Squarespace page with a stock photo of a glowing blue brain and a Calendly link.

Then come the posts. Oh God, the posts. One every morning, at 7:42 a.m. precisely, because some other carpetbagger told them that was the optimal engagement window. Each one written by the very machine they claim to be an expert in, each one structured identically, like a Soviet apartment block of the mind.

"I asked ChatGPT to run my business for a week.

Here's what happened.

(It changed everything.)

🧵👇"

And then twelve one-line paragraphs of pure vapour. The word "leverage" appears six times. The word "unlock" appears nine. There is a diagram with four overlapping circles, and in the middle where they all overlap, in bold, it says VALUE. And at the end, always at the end, the hook: "DM me the word READY and I'll send you my free AI Transformation Playbook."

I DM'd one of them the word READY. I wanted to see the beast in its natural habitat. What came back was a fourteen-page PDF that had very obviously been generated in a single prompt, complete with a section on "Ethical AI Governance" that contradicted itself twice on the same page, and a final slide inviting me to book a "complimentary discovery call", which is the consulting equivalent of a man in a trench coat offering to show you his watches.

## The Discovery Call

I booked the call. Of course I booked the call. I had to know.

He appeared on the screen in front of one of those blurred virtual backgrounds that make a person look like they are being interviewed in witness protection. He was enormous with enthusiasm. He had the wide, glassy, unfocused eyes of a man who had been on four of these calls already that morning and had sold nothing, and who was beginning to suspect, somewhere deep in the reptile part of his brain, that the whole thing might be about to fall apart.

"So tell me," he said, leaning in, "where are you on your AI journey?"

I told him I didn't know I was on a journey. I told him I just wanted to know whether any of this stuff would actually help, and how, and what it would cost, and what would happen when it broke.

He did not like this. You could see it pass across his face like a cloud over a car park. These people do not deal in questions with answers. They deal in Readiness Assessments and Maturity Models and Capability Roadmaps, a whole jargon-encrusted cosmology designed to keep you in a permanent state of not-quite-ready, forever on the brink of transformation but never actually transformed, because a client who has been transformed stops paying.

"What I'm hearing," he said, which is what they always say right before they tell you what they were going to say anyway, "is that you need a strategy."

I asked him what he'd built with it. Himself, personally. With his own two hands.

There was a long pause. Somewhere on his end, a dog barked.

"I advise at the executive level," he said.

## The Dancefloor

And that is the heart of it, the rotten black pit at the centre of the whole thing. The AI dancefloor is vast. It is cavernous, echoing, infinite, a great neon warehouse rave with the bass turned up so loud you can't hear yourself think, which is precisely the point. And there is enough space on that floor for every talentless asshole in the land to still get up and try to strut their stuff.

Nobody can see the steps properly in this light. The music is too loud for anyone to notice you've no rhythm. And the people paying the cover charge, the bewildered owners of mid-sized logistics firms and regional insurance brokers and family-run manufacturing outfits who've been told by the newspapers and their nephews and their golf partners that they'll be dead in eighteen months if they don't "do AI", they are standing at the edge of the floor clutching their chequebooks, terrified, desperate for someone, anyone, to take them by the hand and tell them it's going to be alright.

And the carpetbagger is there. He is always there. Smiling. Holding out a hand. Charging by the hour.

What he sells them, in the end, is not technology. He doesn't understand the technology. He couldn't explain a vector embedding if you held a gun to his head and the gun was also a vector embedding. What he sells is relief. A forty-slide deck that says the word "roadmap" enough times that the client can tell the board they've "got a handle on AI". A pilot project that a junior contractor stitches together from an API and some sticky tape over a long weekend. A workshop, God help us all, a full-day workshop with Post-it notes and a "use case ideation sprint", where the staff are encouraged to brainstorm ways to automate themselves out of a job and then fed a lukewarm lunch.

And then, six months later, when nothing has changed except the bank balance, he's gone. Off to the next one. A new logo on the Squarespace page. A new post at 7:42 a.m. "The #1 reason AI projects fail (and it's not what you think)." It is, of course, exactly what you think. It is him.

## Last Call

I left the conference centre late that afternoon, staggering out into the grey car park with a tote bag full of branded stress balls and the sick, hollow feeling of a man who has looked too long into the abyss and found that the abyss had a Calendly link. Behind me, through the glass doors, I could still hear them. The honking. The swooping. "Agentic." "Unlock." "Transformation." "Let's take this offline."

There's a high-water mark to every one of these madnesses. The dot-com bubble had one. Crypto had one. You can stand on a hill a few years after and look west, and with the right kind of eyes you can almost see it, the place where the wave finally broke and rolled back. This one hasn't broken yet. But it will. They always do. And when it does, when the money dries up and the boards stop asking about AI strategy and start asking where all the money went, the carpetbaggers will fold up their tents, scrub their LinkedIn profiles, and drift off into the night in search of the next great swindle.

Quantum, probably. Keep your wallets close.
