Way back in the day, before there was much in the way of tooling or frameworks to support data lineage tracking, I'd built my own running directly on our Redshift instance. The basic observation that led to this stored procedure being developed is that it's possible to tokenize and then "join" the elements making up an individual query to a list of schema and table names, so that there's a reasonable chance that if a given query mentions my_schema.my_table, then it's referring to that table. 

Then we just need to iteratively run the lineage stored procedure on every query in our main Airflow DAG and we've got ourselves an upstream/downstream lineage matrix. Imagine a huge 2D matrix with the names of each table along both axes and a mark indicating if one table is upstream of another in the lineage graph. Simples. 

Needless to say this was pretty hot stuff after the SysOps team saying it wasn't possible, without some serious 3rd party frameworks and investment. I think it actually embarassed them, as they had told their own bosses much the same only to see me develop and deploy this the next month. 

Come the monthly "Show and Tell", this approach was on the agenda alongside about six other projects. The audience was VP's, Directors down to the individual teams and internal users, maybe 400 people in our New York office, and a few interested joiners from London and Tel Aviv. 

This think needed a name, so I went ahead and called it after what it was there to measure - Blast Radius - as it allowed the measurement of the effect of failure on some critical data infrastructure. 

The presentation went well, a VP was commenting on how great it would be in production, and what a catch name, as it would be great to help with detecting any failures, when some utter fucker of a business analyst interrupted the VP. "I find this name upsetting as my father is the army", said this cnut, and proceeded to start blubbering away on the call. 

There is a particular kind of silence that falls over a call with four hundred people on it, and it is not the silence of a library or a church. It is the silence of a herd of antelope that has just spotted something long and low moving through the grass. Nobody breathed. Nobody unmuted. Somewhere in Tel Aviv a man froze with a falafel halfway to his mouth. The VP, who seconds earlier had been talking about production rollouts with the flushed enthusiasm of a man on his third espresso, now looked like someone who had stepped on a rake in the dark. You could see the calculation happening behind his eyes, the cold actuarial maths of corporate survival - is this a hill, and am I prepared to die on it? The answer, as it always is in these rooms, was no.

“That’s, uh, a really valid point,” he said, and I watched my tool’s name die right there on the screen, gunned down mid-sentence by its own champion. The analyst sobbed on. The chat filled with a slow, horrible drip of heart emojis and “thank you for sharing” messages, each one another nail. Six other projects were waiting their turn, and all of them now had to follow this, like a bar band taking the stage after a fistfight. I said something gracious. I have no idea what. My mouth was moving on autopilot while some deeper, reptilian part of my brain was screaming that I had just built a working lineage engine out of string, regex and pure spite, on a database that was never meant to do it, and the only thing anyone in the building would remember was that it had made someone cry.

The rename came through within the week, delivered with the solemn gravity of a peace treaty. Blast Radius was dead. In its place rose something so beige, so lovingly sanded of every sharp edge, that I physically cannot recall it now, Data Dependency Insights, or Impact Mapping Utility, or some other phrase that sounded like a laxative for spreadsheets. It did exactly the same thing. Same stored procedure, same tokeniser, same giant glorious matrix lighting up like a pinball machine every time someone dropped a table they shouldn’t have. But it no longer had a name that told you what it was for, and in that loss there was a small, sour lesson about how organisations treat clarity as a kind of violence.

Here’s the punchline, and it took me years to fully savour it. Blast radius is not some sinister phrase I dredged up from a munitions manual. It’s bread-and-butter language across the whole cloud and reliability world, printed in architecture whitepapers, spoken calmly in design reviews by engineers at every major tech company on earth, used precisely because it’s the clearest possible way to describe how far a failure spreads. Somewhere, at that very moment, thousands of people were saying it in meetings without a single tear being shed. But not in our New York office. Not on that day. On that day, in that room, the language of engineering ran headlong into the language of feelings, and feelings won on a technical knockout in the first round.

The tool ran in production for years after that, quietly, without ceremony, catching broken pipelines before they took down dashboards that executives would otherwise have screamed about. Nobody ever thanked it by name, mostly because nobody could remember its name. That’s fine. I knew what it was. Every time an upstream table fell over and the matrix lit up showing exactly what was about to burn, I’d allow myself a small private grin and say it under my breath, like a curse, like a prayer, blast radius, you beautiful bastard.

Epilogue

A few years later came the vindication, and it arrived the way all true justice arrives in corporate life - quietly, sideways, buried in a re-org announcement that nobody read past the second paragraph. The same delicate soul who had found a two-word project name too heavy a burden to bear had, it turned out, found the burden of continued gainful employment heavier still. The axe fell. No ceremony, no heart emojis, no four hundred people holding their breath on a call. Just a calendar invite from HR with a vague title, fifteen minutes long, and a laptop collected by courier the following Tuesday.

I heard about it second-hand, over a drink, from someone who still had friends in the New York office, and I’m not proud of what happened next, but I’m not ashamed of it either. I laughed. Not a polite chuckle, but a great ugly bark of a laugh that turned heads at the bar, the laugh of a man who has carried a small grudge in his pocket for years like a smooth stone and has finally found the right window to throw it through. I pictured them at home in the long grey afternoons, free at last to weep freely and without interruption at whatever daytime television had to offer - the property shows, the paternity reveals, the cookery segments where some poor bastard’s soufflé collapses on live TV. Here, at last, was a world built entirely for the easily wounded, a place where nobody would ever say anything as violent as “blast radius” again.

Meanwhile the tool they had so bravely wept against was still running. Still parsing, still tokenising, still joining queries to tables with the dumb faithful persistence of a sheepdog, catching failures in the dead hours of the night and lighting up its great matrix like a switchboard on election night. It had outlived its critic. It had outlived its own name. It would, in all likelihood, outlive me. There is a lesson buried in that somewhere about what lasts in a company and what doesn’t - the work lasts, the sharp tools last, the things that actually do something useful at three in the morning when the pipelines are on fire. The tears, the outrage, the performance of hurt feelings on a public call, those have a shelf life, and it turns out to be shorter than a Redshift cluster’s.

So here’s to you, wherever you are, remote in hand, tissues at the ready. You cost me a name, but you never touched the engine. And somewhere deep in a database schema that nobody has dared to clean up in years, there’s still a stored procedure with a comment at the top that I never got round to changing. It says, in plain block capitals: BLAST RADIUS. Some things you don’t rename. Some things you just let burn.
