It was after my second or third coffee, with the fluorescent lights humming like a hive of sick wasps, that I first understood what I was looking at. Two LinkedIn profiles, side by side on the screen, and a pattern so brazen, so naked in its greed, that I had to doff my cap to its pure insolence.

We'll call them Dakota and Joan. Those aren't their names, and they don't deserve the dignity of real ones in print. They are marketing people. Worse, they are *career* marketing people, the kind who've never once in their lives been asked to make a thing, only to make you *feel* something about a thing somebody else made, and then bill you for the feeling. And they run together as a hiring tag-team.

This is the part the straight marketing world that would never acknowledge the duplicity of this scheme, because the straight marketing world is busy writing "10 Habits of Highly Effective Leaders" and asking itself whether it's okay to use emojis in a cover letter. Dakota and Joan move from company to company *as a pair*. One lands first, always in some vaguely regal post like "Marketing Manager" or "Director of Brand Something", and then, a month or two later, as surely as night follows day and a hangover follows a free bar, the other one slithers in through a side door marked *Strategic Hire*.

It's a running mate arrangement. Same as any presidential campaign: one at the top of the ticket, one on the bottom. The difference is that nobody ever votes for either of them.

You have to understand how these people get elected. It isn't by any democratic process. Nobody in that building ever stood up in a meeting and said, "What this company needs is *another* Director of Marketing, ideally one who's worked with the last one at three previous firms." No. That's not how the machine works.

The way it works is this: Dakota gets in. Dakota spends six weeks in a fog of "listening tours" and "stakeholder alignment sessions", which is corporate code for walking around with a notepad looking worried. Then Dakota discovers, to great and public sorrow, that the department has "a capability gap". A *gap*. A dark and terrible hole in the org chart, through which the whole company might fall screaming into the abyss of missed quarterly targets.

And who could possibly fill such a gap?

Well, as luck would have it, Dakota *knows someone*.

The job spec goes up. It is written with the precision of a tailor fitting a suit to one particular body, listing the exact tools, the exact sectors and the exact obscure certifications that, by some cosmic coincidence, only one person on God's green earth possesses. The "open process" runs for about nine days. A couple of real candidates get dragged through the interview gauntlet as a kind of ritual sacrifice, to make the whole thing look clean for HR. And then Joan arrives, beaming, holding a box of desk plants and a lanyard, and greeting everyone like she's been there for years.

I watched it happen. It was like watching Tammany Hall's men work a convention floor: no fingerprints, no paper trail, just a series of handshakes in a hallway and a sudden, unanimous consensus that nobody can remember agreeing to.

There's an image that has stuck with me ever since, and it has never gone away, not even after the third whiskey on the flight home.

Picture the Serengeti at dusk. Out in the long grass, two jackals. They don't hunt together, exactly; they're too lazy and too cowardly for that. What they do is *wait*. One of them wanders off toward some fat, slow, venture-funded herd, and when it gets lucky and finds a carcass, it yips. And the other one comes running.

That's the whole business model. One strikes lucky so the other one can feed.

They stay a year, maybe two. That's about the half-life of any marketing director's credibility, the time it takes for the board to notice that the "brand refresh" consisted of a new font and a sixty-slide deck with the word *authentic* on every page. Then the pressure builds. Somebody asks a question about pipeline attribution in an all-hands. The CFO starts looking at the agency invoices with the cold, flat eyes of a coroner.

And one of them, Dakota or Joan, it hardly matters which, either gets quietly pushed out the back door with a generous package and a LinkedIn post about "being excited for the next chapter", or leaves first, smelling blood in the water at some fresh, gullible Series B outfit across town.

You already know what happens next. Two months later the other one's LinkedIn lights up: *"Thrilled to announce..."*

Thrilled. Jesus wept.

I'll admit something here that a more responsible corporate navigator would keep quiet about. I would pay real money, *serious* money, the kind of money you only spend in Vegas at four in the morning, for a full data dump of these two's private messages.

I want the WhatsApp threads. I want the Sunday-night voice notes. I want to know what the *deal* is. Is it a blood oath? Shared shame? Some ugly old secret buried under the patio of a 2014 offsite in Florida? Did they once watch a VP fall into a hotel pool and agree never to speak of it, except as leverage?

I have no evidence for any of this, of course. But that's never stopped a rumor from doing honest work in the corporate world, and I can feel this one getting legs already. Somebody in Finance will surely hear it by Thursday.

What I *do* know is that whatever binds them is stronger than loyalty to any employer, any team, any product, any customer, any of the poor bastards they hired underneath them and then abandoned when the music stopped. It is the purest form of political alliance there is: two people who have figured out that the system rewards nothing so much as having a friend on the inside, and who have stopped pretending otherwise. In a way, you almost have to admire it. Almost. The same way you almost have to admire a tapeworm.

Their enquiry came, as these things do, at a moment when I'd let my guard down. I had already handed in my notice. I was a short-timer, a lame duck, floating through my last fortnight in that strange, druggy calm that settles in once you know the swine can no longer touch you. And Joan, sensing weakness the way only a professional can, came sidling up to me in the break room with a coffee she hadn't bought for me and a smile I didn't trust.

"So," she said, all warmth and teeth. "Where are you off to next?"

There it was. The sniff. The jackal nosing at the edge of the carcass, checking for meat. Because of course, a departing employee is just a free reconnaissance drone. Where are they going? Is it hiring? Is there a *gap*? Could Dakota use a new chapter?

I looked her dead in the eye, summoned every last ounce of HR-approved composure I had left, and said:

"I'm not comfortable sharing that information."

It was, in business speak, the most elegant *go fuck yourself* I have ever delivered. I'm told it is still discussed in certain quarters. The smile faltered for half a second, a tiny glitch in the matrix, a flicker of something cold and reptilian behind the eyes, and then she was gone, back across the open-plan office to report to the top of the ticket.

I stood there for a long time afterward with my heart going like a bongo drum, feeling better than I had in eighteen months.

So this is the state of the race. Somewhere out there tonight, in some glass-walled office with a kombucha tap and a ping-pong table nobody uses, Dakota and Joan are already running their next campaign. One of them is on the inside. One of them is waiting by the phone. And some poor, decent hiring manager who genuinely believes in "fair and open processes" is about to get hustled like a tourist in a back-room massage parlor in Tijuana.

There is no moral here. There rarely is. Only a warning, scrawled on the wall of the men's room for whoever comes after:

*When the new Director of Marketing says they've "got just the person" for that new role, check the LinkedIn. Then check it again. Then lock away your budget and run.*

And Joan, if you're reading this, which you are, because you people read *everything* about yourselves.

I'm still not comfortable sharing that information.

Fuck you, Joan.
