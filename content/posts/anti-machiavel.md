I distinctly remember the day when I was introduced to a person that would be perhaps the single greatest career challenge I have had in over 25 years. It was mid April 2018, and I'd been recently minted manager of a team of business analysts and ML engineers for only a few months. I was reporting to a Director of Go-To-Market sales, who reported into our new VP. The more cynical amongst us may call GTM sales as applying "common sense" to try and sell people products they may or may not need. Anyway, my team's job was figuring out if we changed our pricing strategy, churn rate, cross-sell success or improved customer retention, how this would affect long term revenue. In effect, my team made a guess, and wrapped it with enough jargon that we were sure we gave an answer the VP's actually wanted to hear, couched in scientific terms. 

Part of this work involved a number of other teams, and our new VP had replaced some rotten abrasive pig-headed lump of a woman who was fired the previous year and given 15 minutes notice before their access was cut off. Unfortunately, their last act before they got fired was to hire a senior manager responsible for some of the other business analytics teams. The new VP was keen to get us all pulling in the same direction, so he summoned his business analytics team leads out to some unpleasant mid-Western city as it was both the home of this new senior manager, and half way between our New York base and San Francisco where the boss worked from our sales office.

"I think we should really move Bob's team under Alice" - that was literally the first words I'd overheard this new guy say in a work environment. He was less than a week into his new role. He knew neither Bob nor Alice, nor their teams, nor the work they did - but that didn't stop him making a recommendation that might affect a few dozen people's careers and lives. A whim, something to say as one does watching a ballgame, a merely trival sentence uttered without care - a thoughtless, careless exhortation, except one that he crafted so he could pretend to be someone with gravitas, command, even intelligence. This was to be my first introduction to Lehi.

Lehi was a peer manager that became steady source of intense friction over the next couple of years, until all semblance of a working relationship collapsed due to his continued shady backroom deals, empire building and organisational overreach. But it did teach me more about organisational politics than I would have learned in a decade otherwise.

It all ramped up that summer, my immediate boss, a director, transferred to another team, meaning I was going to roll-up reporting directly to the VP. The very fucking day this change was announced, within one hour Lehi had put a meeting into my calendar. 

"Heeeeey buddy" he started, "my team really appreciates all the great work you and your team do in the org". "Thanks Lehi, we try our best to please" I responded, blissfully unaware of what was about to begin, taking his utterances at face value, as a moral support, even, perhaps as a token of appreciation. Not so. Next Lehi dropped the reason for this meeting, and indeed for the prior praise - "so, I've been thinking, maybe it'd be better if you reported directly to me, and I reported to the VP?". I paused, blushed almost at the sheer nerve of it, and fumbled out some words, almost embarassed, that I was happy enough reporting to the same VP thank you all the same. 

What I didn't see at the time is that this hunting expedition of a meeting was almost inevitable - to an empire builder and sociopath, a newly re-positioned team is just some fresh meat to make a play at. Still shocked, I reached out to the VP, asking him if the new org chart had been changed and that Lehi had asked me to report into him. He asked if this was something I wanted, and I said as politely but firmly as possible that it was not something I wanted to see happen. No more was said of this request again, but it was merely the starting gun fired to mark the next two years of political survival. 

Much of the tension was about boundaries and headcount, however initially I had no clue about this. I had a team of eight, he, about 25 people spread over three sub-teams. My first mistake was treating Lehi as my equal, thinking that he was technically competent and relatively honest in his dealings with my both my team as well as our common boss. He was neither technically competent nor honest. 

My team landed some good GTM projects, that made our nut, and raised the profile of the team. This nudged the VP to allocate some additional headcount - two to my team, three to Lehi's. The VP was an old salt, he'd seen politics played out across some of the biggest consulting companies in the western world, and I believe he knew a bullshitter when he saw one. He revealed at our weekly sync that Lehi was complaining about the two extra headcount to my team, notwithstanding the three he got allocated himself. Lehi wanted it all to go to him and his team, as it did two things, it made his team bigger and his scope larger, and diminished mine at the same time. 

This took me several months to chew over - what's the point, why would you want a massively expanded team if you don't land the projects you already have. The answer my friends is the illusion of security and importance that is headcount. Lehi, not being particularly capable one way or the other, could see that being in charge of a larger team made getting rid of him considerably more difficult. Plus undoubtedly it fed ego, and an inflated sense of self-worth. 

By the second year the man had graduated from headcount to infrastructure, and this was where things turned truly weird. Lehi began making noises about his business analysts taking over the infra on-call that my team owned. Now understand, these were people who regarded a Kubernetes pod with the same suspicion a Victorian vicar would regard a bag of interesting mushrooms. They did not want the midnight page. Lehi did not want the page. What Lehi wanted was the keys to the building, because whoever holds the on-call holds the systems, and whoever holds the systems holds the leverage. It was a land grab dressed up as a favor, a hostile takeover with a smile on its face and a fistful of Jira tickets behind its back.

At the same time the backchannel requests started. Quiet DMs to my team start, “quick asks” that were neither quick nor asks, little side quests slipped under the door at odd hours as if my team were his own private militia. So I did the only sane thing a man can do when the bats start circling, I built a fortress out of process. Every request went into a public Slack channel or the formal JIRA intake queue, out in the open, timestamped, visible to God and the VP alike. Sunlight. Lehi hated it the way a guilty man hates CCTV. He kept tunnelling under the walls anyway, and I kept dragging each new tunnel back into daylight, and so it went, week after grinding week, a slow-motion siege conducted entirely through calendar invites and passive-aggressive thread replies.

And make no mistake, the man was good at the dark arts. The finest example was the Rick caper. Rick, one of Lehi’s own reports, had been lined up to lead the Partner business analytics team, reporting straight to the VP, a clean promotion, signed and sealed. Then, like a fool, Rick went out on holiday for a week. By the time he came back sunburnt and relaxed, Lehi had got himself into the VP’s diary and walked out owning both teams. In one week. Rick left a team lead and returned a footnote. It was brilliant in the way a jaguar is brilliant, no imagination required, just patience, timing, and absolutely no remorse once the fangs come out.

Then came the roadmap affair. Word filtered back that there was now a “substantive complaint” that I had failed to get Lehi’s approval on my team’s roadmap. His approval. On my roadmap. It’s hard to describe the particular vertigo of discovering that a man you never reported to has been quietly appointing himself your supervisor in the minds of others. Somewhere up the chain a Director of Analytics role was dangling, unfilled, and I would lie awake at three in the morning with the cold certainty that one day I’d open my laptop to find Lehi’s name above mine on the org chart, grinning down like a gargoyle. That was the fear. That was the real thing that had me grinding my teeth through every weekly sync.

But the wheel turns, and it turns on everybody. The VP let slip that there would be no decision on the Director role while certain teams in the org kept moving at the speed of continental drift. Those teams weren’t Lehi’s, but it hardly mattered, because his team had drifted into a strange limbo of its own, mapped to none of the org’s big projects, floating in space like a satellite nobody remembered launching. All that hoarded headcount, and none of it attached to anything the business actually cared about. The armor turned out to be made of tinfoil. Then the VP confided, quietly, that Lehi might not survive the next re-org, and that one of his two teams would be coming to me. Soon after, word came that the VP’s own boss had checked in on the manager of Lehi’s other team, just to see how he was doing. When the people at the very top of the mountain start asking after the welfare of your direct reports, the vultures are already circling.

And then one autumn morning it was simply over. Lehi was gone, laid off in a re-org, his empire dissolved and parcelled out like the furniture of a bankrupt casino. I had expected to feel triumphant. Instead I felt the strange hollowness of a man who has spent two years bracing against a wall that suddenly isn’t there. But I came out the far side knowing things I could never have learned from any management book - that headcount is not safety, that process is a weapon, that sunlight and honesty beats cunning in the long run, and that the man who spends his days building an empire usually forgets to build anything else.
