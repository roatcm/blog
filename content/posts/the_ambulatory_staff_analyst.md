Every manager inherits somebody. You take over a new team, you sit down with the org chart and a strong coffee, and somewhere among the names there's one that makes your predecessor go quiet and look out the window when you ask about it. A legacy. An heirloom. A piece of furniture that came with the house, and that nobody quite had the heart, or the paperwork, to throw out.

Mine was Gerry.

Gerry was a staff analyst, grandfathered onto my new team in a reorg that had been drawn up, as far as I could tell, by someone moving boxes around a slide at random while on a conference call about something else. And Gerry was *famous*. Everyone, everywhere in the building knew Gerry. The receptionists knew Gerry. The facilities guys knew Gerry. I'm fairly sure the man who came round on Thursdays to water the office plants knew Gerry, and had at some point been helped by Gerry with a query.

Within a week of taking him on, I'd lost count of the number of people who stopped me in the corridor to say, with a warm and slightly knowing smile, "Oh, you've got *Gerry* now." The way you'd say it to someone who'd just adopted a large and very friendly dog that was known to eat sofas.

## The Wandering Monk

Here's the thing that makes Gerry hard to write about. He was a good man. A genuinely, almost unnervingly good man.

Gerry was a networker, but not the kind I usually write about on this blog. He wasn't one of the political creatures, the operators and courtiers who network the way a vine networks, wrapping itself around anything taller so it can climb a little higher towards the sun. Gerry networked to *help*. He reached out to people because they had a problem and he thought he could fix it, and that was the whole of it. No angle. No scheme. No hidden agenda item buried at the eleventh minute. I checked. Believe me, I checked.

Gerry knew his stuff. He'd been around long enough to have seen most of the stack being built, and he carried a map of the whole company's technical estate around in his head, every dodgy cron job and every table that nobody dared drop. He also held fixed opinions. Fixed the way the Rock of Gibraltar is fixed. Most of these opinions concerned where Rust and Go ought to be deployed in the business, which according to Gerry was *everywhere*, immediately, starting with whatever you happened to be working on at the time he wandered past.

And wander he did. Gerry drifted from group to group across the floor like Kwai Chang Caine in *Kung Fu*, walking the earth, sandals and all, turning up in some dusty frontier town just in time to help the downtrodden villagers with their problems before moving on again, at peace, into the sunset. Except the frontier town was the payments team, and the downtrodden villagers were three backend engineers with a memory leak.

He'd help people rewrite their queries. He'd help them redesign their back-end systems. He'd sit with some poor graduate for an entire afternoon untangling a knotty performance problem in code that Gerry had never seen before and would never see again. You'd find him at the far end of the building, hunched over somebody else's monitor, drawing boxes and arrows on a whiteboard with the serene expression of a man who has found his purpose in life.

People loved him for it. Of course they did. Free senior engineering help, on tap, from a man who never asked for anything in return? It was like having a plumber who lived in your attic and came down to fix your boiler for the joy of it.

## The Problem

This wandering, this boundless desire to help everyone else, meant one other thing.

Gerry got absolutely nothing done himself.

I don't mean he was slow. I don't mean he missed the odd deadline. I mean that, in a calendar quarter, the total measurable output of Gerry's own assigned work could have fitted comfortably on the back of a beer mat, with room left over for the phone number of a good divorce lawyer.

I'd assign him something. A modest piece of work. A model to refresh, a pipeline to tidy up, a report that the finance team had been asking about since roughly the Bronze Age. Gerry would nod, enthusiastically, and say something like, "Oh yes, I've got some thoughts on that." And then he'd vanish. Not physically. He was in the building every single day, earlier than me and later than me. But he'd vanish *into the building*, swallowed up by a dozen other teams' problems, and the thing I'd asked for would sit there, untouched, gathering dust and the occasional passive-aggressive email from finance.

At our one-to-ones, I'd ask how it was going.

"Ah. Well. I got pulled into something with the platform team."

The week after, it was the mobile team. Then data engineering. Then some team in marketing I'd never heard of, who it turned out were running a production workload off a laptop under someone's desk, which, to be fair to Gerry, was a genuine emergency.

That was the maddening part. It was *always* a good reason. It was never laziness, never skiving, never a long lunch dressed up as research. Every time, there was a real person with a real problem, and Gerry had helped them, and the business was genuinely better off for it. It's very hard to give someone a bad performance review for being too helpful. It's like scolding a Labrador for bringing you your slippers. You feel like a monster, and the Labrador doesn't understand what it's done wrong.

Meanwhile, I had a headcount line on my budget with the word "Staff" in front of it, a salary attached to it that would make a mid-level manager weep, and a team that was carrying his share of the work on top of their own. And I had a boss who looked at our output every quarter and asked, perfectly reasonably, what exactly my most senior analyst was *for*.

Gerry was, in short, a veritable pain in the ass to manage. A lovely, kind, brilliant pain in the ass.

## The Peacemaker

If the wandering was the problem, the naivety was the thing that broke my heart a little.

Gerry believed, truly believed, in the *business*. Not the company as a collection of fiefdoms and turf wars and people trying to get ahead of each other. The business, in the abstract, as one big shared endeavour where everybody was pulling in the same direction. And so he made it his mission, over and over again, to get teams to work together. For the good of the business.

He'd notice two teams building the same thing, which happened more often than any executive would like to admit, and he'd try to get them in a room together. He'd draw up proposals for shared platforms. He'd suggest that the team with the good data pipeline might open it up to the team with the bad one. He'd write long, thoughtful, beautifully reasoned documents about consolidation and reuse and the dangers of duplicated effort.

And managers like me would sit there, nodding along to all this altruism, knowing in our cold little hearts that it was about as useful as negotiating a truce between Ohio State and Michigan, and asking both teams to agree to share the spoils every gameday. Sure, Gerry. Great idea. Let's get the Buckeyes and the Wolverines to split the trophy down the middle and go for a beer afterwards.

Because those two teams building the same thing? They weren't doing it by accident. Each of them was doing it *on purpose*, because their respective directors were locked in a slow-motion knife fight over who'd own that capability next year, and the headcount and budget that came with it. Consolidation meant one of them losing. Reuse meant one of them admitting the other had built it better. Nobody in that room was going to do either, not for Gerry, not for the business, not for God himself if He turned up with a slide deck.

Gerry never quite saw it. Or if he saw it, he refused to believe it, which is a different and more admirable kind of blindness. He'd walk out of each failed peace summit puzzled and a little wounded, like a man who'd offered a bag of sweets to a pack of wolves and couldn't work out why they'd bitten his hand.

## The Door Opens

He made one push for promotion in my time managing him.

It came out of nowhere, at the end of a one-to-one that had, until that point, been about the finance report. Again. He mentioned, a little shyly, that a role was opening up over in engineering for a "Distinguished Engineer," and he thought he might like to apply for it. Or, if that wasn't possible, maybe he could request that promotion in place, here, on my team.

I want to be honest about what happened next, because I'm not proud of it, and this blog is supposed to be about the truth.

The second part of that sentence, the bit about a promotion in place, I let float gently past me, like a leaf on a river. The first part, I grabbed with both hands.

Seeing the door opening a crack, I eagerly pushed him through it. I lavished praise. I lavished goodwill. I lavished encouragement with the enthusiasm of a man helping his brother-in-law pack his car after a very long Christmas visit.

*Gerry, that's fantastic. You'd be perfect for it. Honestly, it's exactly the right fit. Don't you worry, we'll figure something out. Shucks, it's going to be hard to see you go, but we'll somehow manage in time.*

I wrote him a reference that would have embarrassed a eulogy. I talked him up to the engineering director at every opportunity. I may, at one point, have used the word "visionary."

And here's the part that still makes me laugh, a little ruefully, whenever I think about it. Every word I said was *true*. He was brilliant. He did know more about the company's technical estate than anyone alive. He had helped half the engineering floor at one point or another, and they all loved him. On paper, and in person, Gerry was a far better fit for a role whose whole job was to wander the building dispensing wisdom than he ever was for a role whose job was to deliver the finance report on time.

The only lie was in the *why*. I wasn't pushing Gerry towards that door because it was the right thing for Gerry. I was pushing him because it was the right thing for my budget, and my team, and my own sanity. That it happened to be the right thing for Gerry as well was a lucky coincidence that let me sleep at night.

He got the role. Of course he got the role. Half the people on the interview panel owed him a favour, and the other half had been helped with a query at some point. And as far as I know, he's still over there to this day, wandering from team to team like a monk in sandals, telling everyone they should rewrite it in Rust. Only now it's his actual job, and somebody has to sit through a meeting once a year and call it "technical leadership."

## What Gerry Taught Me

I've managed a lot of people since Gerry, and a few of them have been Gerrys, in their own way. Brilliant, kind, helpful, and almost entirely unproductive on the things they were actually being paid for. If you've got one on your team right now, here's what I wish someone had told me.

**Helpfulness is not a job description.** Senior people helping others is wonderful, and every team should have someone like that. But if all of someone's impact lands on other people's scorecards and none of it lands on yours, you're running a free consultancy on your own headcount budget. Somebody, eventually, is going to ask why.

**Make the invisible work visible.** Half my problem with Gerry was that nobody could see what he was doing, me included. Get your wanderer to keep a list of who they helped and how. You'll either discover they're the most valuable person in the building, which gives you a case for a different role, or you'll discover that a lot of it was whiteboard philosophy, which gives you a very different kind of conversation.

**Never mistake a political problem for a technical one.** Gerry thought two teams building the same thing was a failure of coordination. It wasn't. It was a turf war, and turf wars aren't settled by good architecture documents. If you want to fix duplication in a company, start with whoever controls the budgets, not the code.

**Find the role that fits the person.** The best thing I ever did for Gerry, I did for the wrong reasons. But it was still the best thing. Some people are square pegs who'll never fit your round hole no matter how hard you push, and the kindest move is to go and find them a square one. Ideally before you've wasted two years of both your lives trying.

**And be honest with yourself about why you're doing it.** I got lucky. My selfish motive and Gerry's best interests happened to point in the same direction. They won't always. When a manager is suddenly very, very keen on someone's career move, it's worth asking who it's really for. Especially when that manager is you.

I bumped into Gerry a couple of years later, at the coffee machine on the engineering floor. He looked happier than I'd ever seen him. He told me all about a team he'd been helping that week, and their terrible database schema, and how they really ought to be thinking about Go for the ingestion layer.

Then he asked whether that finance report had ever got finished.

It hadn't. It never did.
